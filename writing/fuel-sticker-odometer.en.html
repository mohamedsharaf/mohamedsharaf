On 1 November Egypt’s Ministry of Petroleum started a one-month trial in Port Said of a system that tracks fuel through each car’s electronic traffic sticker. Stations read the sticker to log every fill-up, and one stated goal is spotting unusual consumption. The ministry says it won’t limit quantities or touch prices.

Someone asked me a fair question: what stops a driver filling up, siphoning the fuel out, selling it and coming back? The sticker proves which car it is. It doesn’t prove the car burned the fuel.

My answer: scan the sticker and photograph the odometer. Fuel taken against kilometres driven, checked over several fill-ups, shows who takes far more than their car could use. 50 km since the last visit and 40 litres requested is worth a look.

The idea is simple. Making it hard to cheat is the real work:

  • Take the photo live on the station’s device, with a signed time and device ID. Never accept an upload from the driver’s phone.
  • Read the number plate automatically and match it to the sticker, so a sticker can’t move to another car.
  • Treat any odometer reading lower than the last one as an instant alert.
  • Tie every transaction to the attendant who made it. Colluding staff are the biggest risk, and their patterns stand out.
  • Flag, don’t block. Broken odometers and long trips are real, so a person makes the call.
  • Keep the reading, delete the photo.

This is my own proposal, not a description of how the official system works.
