02 / Field notes

Things I’ve seen.
Things I learned.

Five stories from three decades of servers, systems and security. No client names, and the details are blurred on purpose. The lessons are real.

01The server that started talking to strangersHosting · Incident response+
The situation

Run hosting long enough and it happens: a customer’s server starts sending traffic it has no business sending. Usually at night. Usually on a weekend.

What I did

Contain first, ask questions second. Cut it off, keep the evidence, find the way in, often an old forgotten script. Then rebuild clean and close that same hole on every other server, not just this one.

What stayed with me

Incidents aren’t solved at 3 a.m. They’re solved by what you prepared before 3 a.m.

02The backup nobody had testedInfrastructure · Resilience+
The situation

Everyone says they have backups. Far fewer people have ever restored one. You find out which kind you are on the day a disk dies.

What I did

Treat the restore as the real product. Scheduled restore tests, copies in more than one place, and a short written plan for what comes back first and who does what.

What stayed with me

A backup you’ve never restored is a hope, not a plan.

03100,000 people talking at once. In 2005.Hosting · Real-time scale+
The situation

By 2005 we were running around 500 websites and more than 2,000 voice chat rooms. On a busy night, 50,000 to 100,000 people were live at the same moment, talking over the slow home internet of the time. Just our own servers, our own code, and us.

Didn’t exist yet:CloudAutoscalingNode.jsGoRustDockerKubernetes

We made it work anyway :)

What I did

Plan for the busiest night, not the average one. Spread the rooms across servers so one failure never took everyone down. Watch the load all evening, keep abusive users out, and keep the setup simple enough to fix while people were still talking.

What stayed with me

People forgive slow. They don’t forgive gone.

04The test that found the front door openPenetration testing · Audits+
The situation

Teams expect a security test to find something clever. Most of the time it finds something simple that has been sitting there for years.

What I did

Default passwords, admin panels open to the whole internet, old software nobody owned anymore. Test like an attacker, then write it up so a busy manager can act on it: what’s wrong, how bad it is, what to fix first.

What stayed with me

Fix the boring things first. Attackers love boring.

05Security on the whiteboard, not the checklistSecure systems · Large organisations+
The situation

Big systems with many users and sensitive data, where a mistake isn’t just a bug. It’s a headline, and sometimes a lot worse.

What I did

Bring security into the design meeting instead of the launch checklist: who can see what, what gets logged, what happens when a part fails, and how we’ll prove all of it later.

What stayed with me

The cheapest security fix is the one you make on the whiteboard.

Every story is shared with the people involved kept anonymous. That’s how I’d treat yours, too.
