Almost every company tells me it has backups. Far fewer can tell me when they last restored one. That gap is where the bad days come from: the job that silently stopped months ago, the backup that holds the database but not the uploaded files, the encryption key that only existed on the server that just died.

Why backups fail quietly

  • The job stopped. A password changed, a disk filled up, a script broke after an update. Nobody reads the email that says “failed”.
  • The wrong things are saved. The database is there, but not the files, the configuration, the certificates or the cron jobs that make the system work.
  • The backup can be deleted. If the server can reach its backups with write access, so can ransomware that takes over the server. Attackers now look for backups first.
  • Nobody knows how to restore. The person who set it up left, and the steps were in their head.

The 3-2-1 rule, with one addition

Keep 3 copies of your data, on 2 different kinds of storage, with 1 copy off-site. Today I would add: at least one copy that can’t be changed or deleted for a set period. That is called immutable storage, and most cloud storage services offer it as object lock or a retention setting. It is the copy ransomware can’t touch.

Decide what “good enough” means

Two questions, answered by the business and not only by IT:

  • How much data can we lose? If the answer is “one hour”, a nightly backup is not enough.
  • How long can we be down? Restoring two terabytes over a slow link can take a day. Better to learn that now.

Test restores on a schedule

  1. Every month, restore a few random files and one database to a separate machine, and open them.
  2. Every quarter, restore a whole system from scratch, following written steps, ideally done by someone other than the person who wrote them.
  3. Time it, and compare with the downtime the business said it can accept.
  4. Write down what went wrong and fix the steps. Every test teaches you something.

Small things that matter

  • Alert on failure and on silence: “no backup report today” should be an alarm too.
  • Store encryption keys and passwords for the backups somewhere that survives the loss of the main systems.
  • Back up the cloud too. Your email and file-sharing provider protects its platform, not your deleted folder from last month.

A backup is only finished when you have restored it. Until then, it is a hope with a monthly bill.
