Here is the honest situation in most companies right now: people are already using AI chatbots at work, whether there is a policy or not. They paste in emails, contracts, customer lists, error logs and source code, because it saves them time. Banning it rarely works; it just moves to personal phones. The better question is how to use it without leaking things you can’t take back.

Where the data actually goes

When someone pastes text into an AI tool, three things matter:

  • Is it used for training? Many free and personal plans may use conversations to improve the model unless you switch that off. Business and enterprise plans usually don’t, and say so in their terms.
  • How long is it kept? Even without training, chats are stored for some time, for abuse monitoring or simply as history.
  • Who can see it? Your own staff (shared workspaces), the vendor’s staff in limited cases, and anyone who later gets into that account.

None of this is hidden. It is in each provider’s privacy and data terms. Read them for the tools you approve, not for every tool on the internet.

A policy people will actually follow

  1. Pick the approved tools. One or two, on a business plan, with training turned off and single sign-on. Make the approved option easier than the unofficial one.
  2. Sort data into three groups.
    • Fine: public information, general questions, drafts with no names.
    • Only in approved tools: internal documents, code, non-sensitive business data.
    • Never: passwords and API keys, customers’ personal data, health or payment data, anything under a confidentiality agreement.
  3. Strip before you paste. Replace names, emails, account numbers and server addresses with placeholders. The answer is usually just as useful.
  4. A human checks the output. AI is confident even when it is wrong. Anything that goes to a customer, into production or into a contract gets read by a person first.
  5. Say where to ask. One named person or channel for “can I use AI for this?”. Most leaks happen because nobody knew whom to ask.

When the data is too sensitive

For some work the right answer is to keep the model inside your walls. Open models you run on your own server, or a private cloud deployment with clear data terms, let you use AI on sensitive material without sending it to a public service. It costs more effort, so keep it for the cases that need it.

Connecting AI to your systems

The next step many teams take is letting an AI assistant read their email, drive or ticket system. That is powerful, and it changes the risk: the assistant can now see everything its account can see. Give it the narrowest access possible, separate accounts for separate jobs, and logs of what it reads and does.

Used well, AI saves real hours every week. The goal is not to stop people using it. It is to make the safe way the easy way.
